Refresh / Content Opportunity Scoring

An interpretable prioritization approach for identifying content pages that deserve earlier human review using content age and recent performance trend signals.

Author: Sameer Lane: Refresh / Content Opportunity Scoring Dataset: 30,000 rows September 2026

Abstract

This study asks whether a simple, interpretable scoring approach can prioritize content pages for human review using content age and recent performance trend signals. I used an anonymized FlyRank content refresh dataset containing 30,000 content-level rows and built an age-only baseline before extending it with a negative-trend component. The extended score combines time since the last update with the magnitude of recent negative trend, producing a ranked review queue. Compared with the age-only baseline, the extended score changes prioritization by giving additional weight to pages experiencing stronger recent declines. The result is directional decision-support evidence rather than a causal claim that refreshing a page will improve its performance.

1. Introduction / Problem Statement

Content teams often have more pages to review than they can manually inspect at the same time. A repeatable prioritization method can help editors decide which pages deserve attention first.

The research question for this capstone is:

Can a simple, interpretable scoring approach identify content pages that should be prioritized for human review and possible refresh, using content age and recent performance trend signals?

The unit of analysis is an individual content page. The output is a prioritization score and ranked action queue. The human decision is whether to review, refresh, investigate further, or monitor a page.

The main cost of a wrong decision is wasted editorial effort or delayed attention to a page that may deserve investigation. For this reason, the score is designed as decision support and is not intended to automatically execute content changes.

The analysis uses the anonymized FlyRank content refresh dataset. The primary signals used by the scoring rule are:

  • days_since_last_update
  • trend_pct

Additional fields such as trend_direction, freshness_tier, ctr, and avg_position are used for interpretation and reporting.

Data safety and leakage considerations

Pseudonymous content_id values are used only to identify individual rows and are not used as features.

The analysis does not include client names, domains, private search queries, credentials, or raw private exports in the public paper.

trend_pct and trend_direction are derived performance signals, so they were explicitly considered as potential leakage risks. In this analysis they are treated as observed/current signals for prioritization, rather than as future outcome labels. No future-window outcome is used as a predictive target.

3. Methodology

The method is an interpretable rule-based prioritization score. It was deliberately kept simple so that an editor can understand why a page received a high priority.

Baseline

The transparent baseline uses content age only:

baseline_score = days_since_last_update / 30

Older content therefore receives higher baseline priority.

Extended scoring approach

The extended score keeps the age component and adds the magnitude of recent negative trend:

model_score = days_since_last_update / 30 + abs(negative trend_pct) / 10

Only negative trend_pct contributes to the decline component. Pages are sorted by descending score, with days_since_last_update used as a secondary ordering signal.

Implementation

model_df["score"] = (
    model_df["days_since_last_update"].fillna(0).clip(lower=0) / 30
    + model_df["trend_pct"].fillna(0).clip(upper=0).abs() / 10
)

This is a prioritization rule, not a trained predictive model. There is no probability estimate and no claim that the score predicts future performance.

5. Evaluation

This capstone is a ranking and decision-support analysis rather than a supervised prediction task. Therefore, classification metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, and AUC are not appropriate for the current scoring method.

The evaluation instead compares how the extended score changes the ranking relative to the transparent age-only baseline on the same dataset.

Evaluation framing: The measured result is a change in prioritization ranking. It is not prediction accuracy and does not measure causal business impact.

No randomized experiment was performed. Consequently, the analysis cannot determine whether refreshing a high-ranked page would actually improve search performance.

6. Interpretation

The analysis finds that combining content age with recent negative performance creates a stronger prioritization signal than age alone.

The highest-ranked candidates tend to have two characteristics:

  1. They have not been updated for a relatively long period.
  2. They show a strongly negative recent performance trend.

For example, the highest-ranked candidate has 373 days since its last update and a trend of -100. Its baseline score is 12.433, while the extended score is 22.433.

The important interpretation is not that these pages are guaranteed to need refreshing. Instead, the scoring approach provides a transparent way to decide which pages deserve earlier investigation.

A key negative result is that the analysis does not demonstrate causal refresh impact. A high score does not prove that changing the page will improve its future performance.

7. Ranked Recommendations

The output can be used as an editorial review queue.

  1. Start with the highest-ranked pages.
  2. Review current content quality, search intent, and relevance.
  3. Check whether the observed decline is meaningful.
  4. Decide whether to refresh, investigate further, or monitor.
  5. Track the decision and reassess performance over time.

Human review is required. The score must not automatically trigger a content refresh. Editorial judgment is necessary because the dataset does not represent every factor that may influence search performance.

The current baseline assigns a positive prioritization score across the dataset because the formula incorporates age and/or negative trend. Therefore, the result should be understood as a continuous ranking of review priority rather than a claim that every page requires refreshing.

Confidence is moderate for the narrow task of ranking pages using the selected signals. Confidence is low for any claim about causal refresh impact or universal search behavior.

8. Limitations & Honest Framing

  • The scoring method is an interpretable prioritization baseline, not a causal model.
  • Only content age and recent negative trend are used in the scoring formula. Other potentially relevant factors are omitted.
  • The baseline comparison measures ranking differences rather than business impact.
  • The dataset is observational and anonymized, so the findings should be treated as directional rather than universal.
  • Human review is required before taking editorial action.
  • The scoring approach should be revalidated if the data distribution, content strategy, search environment, or business objective changes.
  • No claim is made that this analysis predicts Google's ranking algorithm.

10. Conclusion

This capstone demonstrates a transparent and repeatable approach for prioritizing content pages for human review. Adding recent negative performance trend to content age produces a ranking that emphasizes pages showing both staleness and decline.

The main value of the approach is explainability: an editor can see the signals behind a page's priority rather than receiving an opaque model prediction.

The result should remain a decision-support tool. Further validation, domain review, and controlled experimentation would be required before making claims about the causal effect of content refreshes.
